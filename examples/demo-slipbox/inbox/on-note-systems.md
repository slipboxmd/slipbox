# On Note Systems

A note system is a machine for thinking with more than one brain's worth of
working memory. Most people treat notes as storage — a place to put things so
they can stop holding them. But the interesting systems treat notes as a medium
for developing thought over time. The difference is not the software. It is a
handful of ideas about how knowledge accumulates.

## The forgetting problem

We forget almost everything we read. Not because we are careless, but because
memory is metabolically expensive and the brain is ruthless about pruning what
it does not use. Within a day, most of the detail of a book is gone; within a
week, often the argument too. What remains is a vague sense that we once
understood something, and the mild guilt of not being able to reconstruct it.

Highlighting does not fix this. Underlining a sentence feels like learning, but
it is mostly a way of deferring the work of understanding to a later self who
never arrives. The highlight marks the spot where thinking should have happened
and did not. A note system exists, first, to catch what would otherwise
evaporate — but only if the catching involves real cognitive work, not the
illusion of it.

## Atomic notes

The core unit of a good system is the atomic note: one idea, stated completely,
in your own words. Atomicity matters because ideas are recombined, and you can
only recombine things that are separable. If a note contains three ideas welded
together, it can only ever be filed in one place and retrieved for one purpose.
Split those three ideas into three notes and each can be linked, cited, and
reused independently, in contexts you cannot yet foresee.

Writing atomically also forces a kind of honesty. It is easy to nod along with a
paragraph; it is hard to restate its single claim in a sentence you would defend.
The constraint of one-idea-per-note surfaces the places where you did not
actually understand what you read. That friction is the point. The note is not
the output of understanding — writing the note is where the understanding
happens.

## Links over hierarchy

The instinct of most people organizing information is to build a hierarchy:
folders inside folders, a taxonomy decided in advance. Hierarchies are seductive
because they feel orderly, but they are brittle. Every note must live in exactly
one place, and you must decide that place before you know how the note will be
used. The taxonomy you design on day one is a prediction about a future you have
not lived yet, and it is almost always wrong.

Links are the alternative. Instead of asking "which folder does this belong in,"
you ask "what does this connect to." A note can link to any number of others, and
those links carry the actual intellectual work: this idea supports that one, this
one contradicts that one, this example instantiates that principle. The structure
of your knowledge becomes a graph rather than a tree — and a graph can represent
relationships a tree cannot.

## Emergence of structure

If you do not impose a hierarchy up front, where does structure come from? It
emerges. As notes accumulate and link to one another, clusters form around ideas
you return to. Some notes become hubs, linked from many directions; these are
usually the questions or concepts that matter most to you, revealed rather than
declared. A map of a topic can then be written after the fact, as an index into
a region of the graph that grew dense on its own.

This is a slower and stranger way to get organized, and it requires tolerating a
period where the collection looks like a mess. But bottom-up structure has a
property top-down structure lacks: it reflects how you actually think, not how
you imagined you would think before you started. The system teaches you what your
interests are by showing you where the graph thickened.

## Writing as thinking

The deepest claim behind these systems is that writing is not the recording of
thought but the medium of it. We do not think clearly and then write down the
result; we write in order to think clearly, and discover in the writing what we
did not know we believed. A vague idea held in the head can feel profound
precisely because it is vague. Forced onto the page as a complete sentence, it
either sharpens into something real or dissolves into the platitude it always
was.

This is why a note system is not a filing cabinet. A filing cabinet stores
finished thoughts. A note system is where thinking is done — each note a small
act of clarification, each link a small act of synthesis. The accumulated notes
are less an archive than a conversation partner, one that remembers everything
you have worked out and can surprise you by connecting two things you filed years
apart.

## Against the collector's fallacy

The failure mode of every note system is collection without processing. It is
enormously satisfying to save an article, clip a quote, or bookmark a paper. The
saving feels like progress, and the growing pile feels like wealth. But an
unread saved article is not knowledge; it is a debt. The collector's fallacy is
mistaking the act of acquiring information for the act of understanding it.

The antidote is to touch nothing you will not process. A source that is worth
saving is worth reading closely enough to extract at least one atomic note in
your own words; a source that is not worth that is not worth saving. The measure
of a note system is never how much it contains. It is how much of what it
contains you have actually thought about — and how often the system hands you
back an idea, at the right moment, that you would otherwise have lost.
